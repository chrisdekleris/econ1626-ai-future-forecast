How much will AI lift Australia's productivity by 2030?

A forecast of what artificial intelligence could add to Australian productivity by 2030, and why the answer depends mostly on how quickly small businesses adopt it.

The range of estimates

How much will artificial intelligence add to Australia's productivity growth? The studies reviewed by the Productivity Commission (PC) range from 0.05 to 1.3 percentage points a year (Productivity Commission 2025). The Commission's own central case is 0.23 points (Productivity Commission 2025). CommBank's chief economist puts economies that adopt AI "deeply and widely" at 0.8 to 1.0 points (Yeaman 2026). At the bottom, Acemoglu's (2025) ceiling of 0.66 per cent over a decade works out to under 0.07 points a year.

The highest estimate is more than twenty times the lowest. Most of that gap probably comes down to diffusion, meaning how quickly ordinary firms change the way they work to use AI.

Australia's productivity gain from AI by 2030 will depend mostly on how quickly its small firms adopt it. Owning the models or the data centres probably matters less than current policy assumes. Building AI locally is worthwhile when it removes a real barrier to adoption, and Maincode, a Melbourne AI developer, appears to be moving in that direction.

The forecast runs to 2030 because that is the year Jobs and Skills Australia's (2025) fastest adoption pathway reaches 50 per cent. That is long enough for adoption to show up in productivity data, and soon enough to check the forecast against what happens.

Capabilities and economic channels

AI can already affect a large share of work

Eloundou et al. (2024) estimate that around 80 per cent of the US workforce could have at least 10 per cent of their tasks affected by large language models, and by late 2024 nearly 40 per cent of Americans aged 18 to 64 used generative AI (Bick, Blandin & Deming 2024). Bresnahan and Trajtenberg (1995) define a general purpose technology (GPT) as one that spreads across industries, keeps improving and leads to further inventions. AI appears to meet all three tests.

From gains on single tasks to gains for the economy

In a study of 5,179 customer support agents, an AI assistant raised the number of issues resolved per hour by 14 per cent on average and by 34 per cent for less experienced workers, with little effect on experienced staff (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025).

Acemoglu (2025) explains why these gains shrink at the national level. Under Hulten's theorem, the economy-wide gain equals the share of tasks affected multiplied by the average cost saving on them. A 30 per cent saving on 5 per cent of the economy's work is a 1.5 per cent gain, reached gradually as firms adopt. The national gain is therefore roughly the task gain × the share of work actually reorganised, and Australia is weakest on the second.

Small firms are adopting AI slowly

The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) found that around 12 per cent of businesses used AI in 2024–25 (ABS 2026). About 35 per cent of large businesses used AI, up from 9 per cent in 2021–22, compared with around 11 per cent of small and micro businesses (ABS 2026). Small firms that innovate in other ways adopted AI at 19 per cent, and those that do not at 4 per cent (ABS 2026).

The Productivity Commission describes large firms as "adopting and investing heavily" while "small businesses seem to be moving much slower" (Productivity Commission 2025, Box 1.5). Slow technology adoption is a long-running Australian problem, and one reason productivity growth in the decade to 2020 was the slowest in 60 years (Productivity Commission 2025).

Bresnahan and Trajtenberg (1995) warned that decentralised economies use GPTs "too little, too late", because users and suppliers cannot coordinate their investment. Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson (2021) show that using a GPT well requires intangible investment, such as redesigned processes and retrained staff, which national accounts barely record, so measured productivity can fall before it rises. Factories took decades to reorganise around the electric motor (David 1990).

A Treasury note reported that about two-thirds of Australian companies use AI "in some form", but fewer than one in ten describe that use as "significant" (Sadler 2026). Most firms seem to be experimenting with the tools without yet changing how they operate.

Jobs, wages and who gains

In Acemoglu and Restrepo's (2019) framework, automation displaces workers and lowers labour's share of income unless new tasks re-employ them. So far there is little evidence of displacement. Danish data from the two years after ChatGPT's launch show "precise null effects" on earnings and hours, ruling out effects larger than 2 per cent, even as workers take on new tasks such as AI oversight (Humlum & Vestergaard 2025). Treasury reports there is "not yet evidence of significant labour market impacts" (Treasury 2026).

AI helps less experienced workers most (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025), narrowing the productivity gap between new and experienced staff in the same job. Across the economy, however, Acemoglu (2025) expects the gap between capital and labour income to widen. Exposure also differs by gender: 7 per cent of Australian women work in occupations with high automation exposure, compared with 1 per cent of men (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025).

Testing the "AI maker" claim

Dave Lemphers, founder of Maincode and one of the Week 9 Industry Week speakers, has argued publicly for Australian-made AI. His company's model, Matilda, was in his words "made by Australians, trained in Australia by an Australian-owned company" (Williams 2025). The federal government makes a similar case. Assistant Minister Andrew Charlton argues there is "value in Australia not just being an AI taker, but an AI maker" (Walker 2026).

Three questions test this claim.

Where does the money go? Charlton himself estimates that only "10-odd cents" of each AI dollar goes on energy, and that energy and infrastructure together are "a relatively small proportion" (Walker 2026). Hosting data centres captures little of the value. Most of it goes to whoever controls the scarce models, software and customer relationships, the complementary assets discussed in Week 9.

Does ownership change the productivity calculation? Hulten's formula has two inputs, the gain on each task and the share of work reorganised. Where a model was trained affects neither, so ownership only matters if it makes AI cheaper, more trusted or easier for Australian firms to adopt.

What does it cost? Data centres use about 3 per cent of electricity on the National Electricity Market (NEM), and the Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO) expects 7.8 per cent by 2029–30 (Dux 2026). New data centres would compete for grid capacity with the households and businesses that also need it.

Lemphers' own recent decisions support a narrower version of the maker case. Maincode's new Melbourne facility is built around "advanced, focused models that actually work", aimed at rule-heavy tasks where "anyone who's spent time trying to prompt-engineer a general-purpose model… knows the limits" (iTWire 2025). He has also dropped the "sovereign AI" label as "very divisive" (Williams 2025). Specialised models that follow a bank's compliance rules or a hospital's protocols make AI cheaper to use where general models struggle, and if they reach smaller firms they would lower adoption costs where Australia lags most. Building AI in Australia is most likely to be worthwhile when it makes AI cheaper or easier for Australian firms to use.

Scenarios to 2030

Three scenarios follow, each defined by its policy settings and measured on the same rows in the table below. All gains are in labour productivity. The PC's central case of 2.3 per cent multifactor productivity converts to about 4.3 per cent labour productivity over the decade (Productivity Commission 2025), roughly 0.42 points a year.

Baseline: current policy. The National AI Plan continues unchanged, with its heavy emphasis on infrastructure and data centres (Department of Industry, Science and Resources 2025). Small-firm adoption reaches 50 per cent by 2033, following JSA's steady pathway (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025). AI adds about 0.42 points a year, enough to support Treasury's assumption of 1.2 per cent productivity growth (Treasury 2026) but not exceed it, and large firms pull further ahead. What to watch: small-firm adoption above 25 per cent in 2026–27 would mean adoption is faster than assumed.

Upside: adoption-focused policy. Government helps small firms adopt AI and build skills, and funds Australian AI builders only where they lower adoption costs. Adoption reaches 50 per cent by 2030, following JSA's faster pathway in which AI mainly assists workers (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025). The gain approaches CommBank's 0.8 to 1.0 points a year (Yeaman 2026), and new tasks appear as fast as old ones disappear. What to watch: small-firm adoption below 20 per cent in 2027 would rule this scenario out.

Downside: infrastructure first. Public money goes into Australian-owned data centres and models, extra grid costs are spread across households, and small firms get no targeted help. Adoption is delayed by about five years, the lag JSA models for non-market sectors (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025), and the gain falls to about 0.13 points a year, near the bottom of the PC's range. Clerical jobs disappear before new tasks emerge, hitting women hardest. What to watch: data-centre demand running ahead of AEMO's forecast while small-firm adoption stalls.

Berg's Week 3 exercise asked what an extra 0.5 points of growth a year does over 20 years, and the answer is about 10.5 per cent higher GDP per person. Compared with no AI, the simulator's baseline leaves Australians about 8.7 per cent better off by 2045, the upside 18.2 per cent and the downside 2.6 per cent. The PC's central estimate is worth about $116 billion of extra GDP over a decade (Productivity Commission 2025).

Building data centres ahead of adoption has a cost on the grid. AEMO's high case reaches about 52 terawatt hours by 2035–36, against 34 in the central case (Dux 2026). Data centres take two to three years to build, while new generation takes about six years for a gas turbine and ten or more for nuclear (Berg 2026). Data-centre demand would therefore arrive years before new supply, and years before small-firm adoption raises productivity.
